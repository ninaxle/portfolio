My Capstone Project

PROJECT OVERVIEW

Accelerator is an analog horror game and critical design intervention I designed and developed for my 2026 Capstone project.

It challenges the AI‑first philosophy by telling a story about the costs of overlooking AI safety and digital access in developing AI products. Meanwhile, it critiques the rapid integration of AI technology when applied as a surface-level band-aid to ongoing environmental and social issues.

This project stands out from my other product design work because it doesn’t propose a solution. It steps back from technological solutionism, recognizing that, given ongoing issues like bias, systematic thinking and user‑flow design alone cannot meaningfully resolve the structural forces behind digital exclusion and access at this point in time.

Game Synopsis

It’s 2040. The air is putrid, living pets are extinct, and humans are lonelier than ever. But fear not. Tedbot brings BORK, the cute and lovable AI dog companion! Best of all? They are a low-maintenance, ever-helpful companion who never leaves and lives eternally. They were designed around Isaac Asimov's "Three Laws of Robotics" and for all humans - Except for you.

BORK is watching you and doesn’t think you belong here. You must escape. Fortunately, you are not entirely alone. Someone has been here before you and left something behind. Find their messages. Find the map, and never trust bright lights.

Background

EXAMINING THE MYTH: BIGGER, BETTER = STRONGER

As a UX/UI designer, it's hard to distance myself from the technologies continuously emerging in the AI space. I am not anti-AI, but I find myself increasingly unsettled by the AI-first philosophy and how aggressively new and improved, "better" technology has made its way into our lives. When I see anything AI, I have several questions. For example, do we always need automatic AI summaries? How much privacy, or agency, does talking to an LLM actually grant me, and who benefits from advanced AI?

TECHNOLOGICAL DETERMINISM

A communications course I took made it clear that technology is not experienced equally; how far you can go with technology heavily depends on access and support. I had a fair taste of this from watching my family members struggle to adapt to AI, let alone using “simple” technology to log into important accounts. Regardless, faster machines, convenience, and more data collection are sold as quick fixes for industries like education, democracy, and law enforcement, despite limited evidence of social impact.

Literature Review

[See: in-depth Literature Review and Primary Proposal]

To this day, technology is celebrated for its promise of efficiency, but built on the constant extraction of our labour, data, and creativity, often without informed consent. An example is CAPTCHA; an early technology known for defining humanness through ability, yet fueling AI training.

As a result, newer versions often rely on more complex puzzles, or require users to unknowingly surrender sensitive data to access basic services. CAPTCHA, an AI-facilitated system, has fueled an arms race in which access is granted based on a narrow notion of ability, designed primarily for English-speaking, able-bodied, and digitally fluent users.

Eventually, I researched other AI-facilitated systems such as facial recognition and biometric systems. Failing any of these authorization methods can mean being locked out of online services or denied entry to physical institutions.

Why a Video Game?

Product designers often use gamification to make experiences more enjoyable, but when used in the wrong context, it can easily slip into dark patterns. In cybersecurity and authentication, gamification can legitimize privilege by using ability as the metric for deciding “winners” and “losers,” rewarding the winners with access.

Building on this, I used this dark pattern to critique digital exclusion and the rapid rise of AI‑mediated verification systems. I came to game design as a medium to foreground “ability” as a central site of conflict.

Game Rules

Accelerator is a car-chase game. The player’s mission is to escape to a place where BORK cannot pursue them. Notably, the player drives a car far from being their dream smart car. They must also avoid AI-related obstacles on the road and outspeed BORK, who actively chases their vehicle.

BORK symbolizes the human anxiety of being displaced by capable AI machines. Meanwhile, the driving mechanism represents the challenges and diverse experiences of navigating a digital landscape, where technology can actively work against an individual’s ability to access services and achieve their goals.

Understanding Friction

Drawing from a range of horror titles, I studied how level structure and gameplay mechanics can guide players through a game’s world and narrative. I ended up reconsidering the role of friction in game design.

While product design often aims to minimize friction, game design frequently uses it deliberately to facilitate play after allowing the players to ease into the environment: an idea I focused on while placing obstacles in the game.

Enemy Design

The goal of the enemy design, an AI companion, was to look approachable, yet uncanny. Each iteration of the enemy design was designed as a set of three, undergoing metamorphosis. The design conveyed AI’s deterioration over time, until it reached a final boss form, or a point of no return. I settled for a monkey because I was inspired by their resemblance to humans and “The Monkey’s Paw.” ​

Design Language

COLOUR AND LIGHTING

Many horror games use red or green to communicate violence or decay. The colour psychology in my game diverges from these standards by using green or red to represent hope, life, and truth. Meanwhile, pigmented blues and purples are unnatural to living organisms. They signify a dreadful atmosphere built by technological acceleration, corporate negligence, death, and pollution.

The obstacles have a bright glow and teach the player to avoid blind trust in flashy, new technology.

DIGITAL ILLUSTRATION

To honour my roots in illustration and the RPG horror games I admired playing, I drew the cutscenes in Procreate.

3D MODELLING AND DIGITAL ILLUSTRATION

I 3D modelled the Captchas on Blender; meanwhile, Sam Lampano crafted the enemy 3D model, using the enemy skins I drew in Procreate.

SOUND DESIGN

I added sound to the text and built tension through the absence of sound on the road, with no entities in the environment aside from the player and enemy.

Final Outcomes

On April 24 - 25, I presented this at my graduate showcase, Radiate. The setup was a monitor, keyboard, mouse, and headphones.

Guests actively sought out Accelerator by word of mouth, even when I was away on break. Interestingly, the game’s main appeal became its challenge: “Who is able to outrun the enemy and make it past chapter one?” The demo sparked engaged reactions, with guests having different aspects to praise, such as the jump scare, visual language, illustrations, car control mechanics, and the script.

Reflection

Going into this project, I initially knew nothing about game development; I wasn’t even a gamer. Thus, I worked through the unknown and made it my new area of expertise. I had never used C#, Unity, or Blender before this experience, but I used them all to make my game.

The process involved several nights of researching articles, following tutorials, forums, and working through trial and error. However, I came away with a genuine love for writing, a clearer sense of how to define and scope a large project, and how to weave in visual craft with my technical background.
