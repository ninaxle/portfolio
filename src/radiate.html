Radiate Showcase Website

Radiate is York University's DESN 2026 Grad Show website, built to help 44 design graduates get discovered by employers. I led a team of 7 designers across 8 months and 3 phases, owning product strategy, team processes, and communication between branding, external relations, and web. The site launched April 3rd and now reaches ~3,000 users/month.

The Problem Statement

Grad show sites often reduce students to an identical grid of thumbnails, making it hard for employers to judge fit and easy for strong candidates to get lost.

The Solution

Every Radiate page gives each student a distinct identity through their own profile, interactive contribution credits, and work presented. The site also functions as a full event hub so attendees have everything they need in one place.

User Research

Competitive Analysis

I analyzed competing grad shows as well as award-winning websites to identify what made them stand out design-wise and where there was room for us to do better. One pattern that kept surfacing even the strongest sites was that visual hierarchy buried people; branding dominated over student descriptions, occupation wasn't immediately clear from the student grids, and contact details required hunting. The visual identity came through before any sense of who the students actually were. This gap shaped our direction for Radiate, with every decision made to ensure the person behind the work was easy to find, read, and remember.

Employer Empathy Map

As graduates, we already had firsthand insight into the student experience. So, we focused the empathy map on the employer, whose needs were less obvious but most critical to the graduates' success. Through the empathy mapping exercise, we gathered what a recruiter would need from the site: quickly assessing fit, understanding a student's focus area at a glance, and finding contact details without friction.

Usability Testing

To gauge the overall website experience, I co-designed the usability test questions and ran the synthesis session, grouping feedback from 12 participants across three task areas: first impressions, wayfinding, and the talent search experience.

Overall sentiment was positive and the “would recommend to a friend” score came in at 4.3 out of 5. But two critical issues emerged from the affinity mapping session: a 73% misclick rate on location, and a recurring sense that pages felt disconnected rather than part of one system.

[FIG.9 "ON A SCALE OF 1-5, HOW LIKELY ARE YOU TO RECOMMEND THE WEBSITE TO A FRIEND?"]

The Project Page A/B Test

To resolve layout direction for student profiles, we ran an A/B preference test.

Screen A won the preference vote by a small margin. Participants also preferred specific details from Screen B, most notably placing the project title above the hero image, which made it immediately clear they were meant to scroll.

Between both layouts, users reported that project descriptions felt overwhelming as a single dense block, and images felt too large, making scrolling tedious. The overall feedback pointed to wanting content broken into smaller pieces so text and visuals could build naturally upon scrolling, leading to a rework of the project page layout in Phase 3 (Screen C).

Addressing Key Pain Points

We worked systematically to address other pain points from the usability test.

Information Hierarchy & Wayfinding

The Problem: When asked, "On a scale of 1 to 5, how easy it was to find details about our location?" 5/12 participants reported the ease as 2. A 73% misclick rate occurred because location details were buried in the footer.

The Fix: Made location details more visible in the updated navbar and footer, with additional touchpoints linking out to the Google Maps page.

Typography & Legibility

The Problem: Several testers flagged that the brand's signature glow effect on the display typeface, combined with tight kerning and motion, made body text difficult to read.

The Fix: Adjusted the colour values to soften the glow radius, improving readability and achieving WCAG compliance without compromising brand identity.

Spacing & Layout Cohesion

The Problem: Excessive negative space caused related data points to feel visually disconnected. Inconsistent use of spacing and border radius across designers also made the overall design feel disjointed.

The Fix: Reworked sections with excessive negative space. Established uniform spacing and radius standards by section within the design system, and tightened layout bounding boxes to group related items by proximity.

Scaling Fixes into the Design System

I worked with the web team to bake these usability fixes directly into our global design system tokens. This ensured that a fix applied to a single layout automatically updated across all 44 live student portfolios. By forcing these updates through the global design system rather than treating them as one-off page edits, we eliminated visual drift across concurrent builds and protected our hard launch timeline.

Building and QA

MY ROLE IN THE BUILD

Drawing on my experience with web specs and frameworks like Tailwind, I checked implementation details and led QA; I reviewed every page against Figma for accuracy across mobile through desktop, standardized layouts where appropriate, built the carousel with Javascript, and occasionally updated CMS content. With the designs finalized, my role was keeping the web team aligned and making sure our work stayed consistent with the quality we'd set out to deliver.

Standards before building

Before anyone started building, Sam Lampano and I led a team readout on Webflow conventions and best practices. Getting this agreed on upfront meant 8 people building in the same file wouldn't quietly drift into 8 different conventions.

What went smoothly

Webflow's flex-based layout combined with our utility-class system made most of the build straightforward. Additionally, standardizing our units meant responsive behavior mostly "just worked" once a section was built correctly the first time.

Technical Trade-offs

Live vectors rendered inconsistently across browsers, so we initially swapped them for baked gradient images. While this worked for static pages, it broke scalability across 44 dynamic CMS pages. Rather than shipping something inconsistent at scale, I cut gradients from CMS pages entirely.

Final Outcomes

Upon shipping, The site has reached roughly ~3,000 users per month, serving as the primary touchpoint between the Class of 2026 and the employers, peers, and industry professionals discovering their work. After the event was over, we updated the gallery to represent people’s booths at the exhibition.

Reflection

The hardest part of this project was coordination across two different axes.

The Cross-Functional Axis: Teams like branding, external relations, and web development each brought competing priorities and timelines. Success meant acting as the bridge to keep everyone aligned.
The Human Axis: Leading a cohort of peers means navigating unpredictable schedules as students juggle intense academic workloads and life.

I learned that leading well under these constraints requires discarding assumptions. It meant moving away from assuming people automatically knew how to execute a task, and treating project outcomes as a shared responsibility.

Crucially, it taught me to design workflows around people's actual capacity, not just their technical capability. By intentionally checking in on a teammate's emotional and bandwidth before assigning a task, we prevented people from becoming quietly overcommitted. This empathy-driven approach smoothed out our handoffs and kept the team united, engaged, and supported from start to finish.
