Radiate Showcase Website

Radiate is York University's DESN 2026 Grad Show website, built to help 44 design graduates get discovered by employers. I led a team of 7 designers across 8 months and 3 phases, owning product strategy, team processes, and communication between branding, external relations, and web. The site launched April 3rd and now reaches ~3,000 users/month.

The Problem Statement

Grad show sites often reduce students to an identical grid of thumbnails, making it hard for employers to judge fit and easy for strong candidates to get lost.

The Solution

Every Radiate page gives each student a distinct identity through their own profile, interactive contribution credits, and work presented. The site also functions as a full event hub so attendees have everything they need in one place.

Competitive Analysis

I analyzed competing grad shows as well as award-winning websites to identify what made them stand out design-wise and where there was room for us to do better. One pattern kept surfacing even in the strongest sites: visual hierarchy burying the people. Branding dominated over student descriptions, occupation wasn't immediately clear from the student grids, and contact details required hunting. You'd absorb the visual identity before you understood who anyone actually was. This gap shaped our direction for Radiate, with every decision made to ensure the person behind the work was easy to find, read, and remember.

Employer Empathy Map

As graduates, we already had firsthand insight into the student experience. So, we focused the empathy map on the employer, whose needs were less obvious but most critical to the graduates' success. Through the empathy mapping exercise, we gathered what a recruiter would need from the site: quickly assessing fit, understanding a student's focus area at a glance, and finding contact details without friction. Strong portfolios alone wouldn't be enough if the person behind the work wasn't visible.

Usability Testing

To gauge the overall website experience, I co-designed the usability test questions and ran the synthesis session, grouping feedback from 12 participants across three task areas: first impressions, wayfinding, and the talent search experience

Overall sentiment was and the “would recommend to a friend” score came in at 4.3 out of 5. But two critical issues emerged from the affinity mapping session: a 73% misclick rate on location, and a recurring sense that pages felt disconnected rather than part of one system.

The Project Page A/B Test

To resolve layout direction for student profiles, we ran an A/B preference test.

Screen A won the preference vote (58% to 42%), though participants pulled specific details from Screen B, most notably placing the project title above the hero image, which made it immediately clear they were meant to scroll.

However, two issues surfaced across both versions: project descriptions felt overwhelming as a single dense block, and images were too large, making scrolling tedious. Both pointed to wanting content broken into smaller pieces so text and visuals build naturally as you scroll.

After the event was over, we updated the gallery to represent people’s booths at the exhibition.

Reflection

The hardest part of this project was coordination across two different axes.

The Cross-Functional Axis: Teams like branding, external relations, and web development each brought competing priorities and timelines. Success meant acting as the bridge to keep everyone aligned.
The Human Axis: Leading a cohort of peers means navigating unpredictable schedules as students juggle intense academic workloads and life.

I learned that leading well under these constraints requires discarding assumptions. It meant moving away from assuming people automatically knew how to execute a task, and instead treating project outcomes as a shared responsibility.

Crucially, it taught me to design workflows around people's actual capacity, not just their technical capability. By intentionally checking in on a teammate's emotional and temporal bandwidth before assigning a task, we prevented people from becoming quietly overcommitted. This empathy-driven approach smoothed out our handoffs and kept the team united, engaged, and supported from start to finish.
