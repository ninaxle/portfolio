Radiate Showcase Website

Radiate is York University's DESN 2026 Grad Show website, built to help 44 design graduates get discovered by employers. I led a team of 7 designers across 8 months and 3 phases, owning product strategy, team processes, and communication between branding, external relations, and web. The site launched April 3rd and now reaches ~3,000 users/month.

The Problem Statement

Grad show sites often reduce students to an identical grid of thumbnails, making it hard for employers to judge fit and easy for strong candidates to get lost.

The Solution

Every Radiate page gives each student a distinct identity through their own profile, interactive contribution credits, and work presented. The site also functions as a full event hub so attendees have everything they need in one place.

User Research

Competitive Analysis

I analyzed competing grad shows as well as award-winning websites to identify what made them stand out design-wise and where there was room for us to do better. One pattern that kept surfacing even the strongest sites was that visual hierarchy buried people; branding dominated over student descriptions, occupation wasn't immediately clear from the student grids, and contact details required hunting. The visual identity came through before any sense of who the students actually were. This gap shaped our direction for Radiate, with every decision made to ensure the person behind the work was easy to find, read, and remember.

Employer Empathy Map

As graduates, we already had firsthand insight into the student experience. So, we focused the empathy map on the employer, whose needs were less obvious but most critical to the graduates' success. Through the empathy mapping exercise, we gathered what a recruiter would need from the site: quickly assessing fit, understanding a student's focus area at a glance, and finding contact details without friction.

Building Trust Through Design Systems at Goodself

Goodself is a healthtech startup that combines social media with evidence-based health information, connecting users with vetted health professionals through interactive streams, courses, and communities. As a UX designer on the product team, I balanced day-to-day feature sprints with building Goodself’s first formal design system.

[ROLE]

UX Designer

[TIMELINE]

Sept 2024 – Apr 2025

[SCOPE]

iOS, Mobile, Tablet, Desktop

[DESIGN SYSTEM DELIVERABLES]

The Challenge

When I joined Goodself, a fast-moving healthtech startup, speed had taken priority over scalability. Design decisions lived in individual heads instead of shared documentation, our brand colour and typography were fragmented, and most interfaces had never been evaluated against WCAG 2.2 AA.

Around the same time, leadership raised a separate but related problem; new-hire feedback kept flagging Goodself's navigation, especially on desktop, as clunky and unintuitive and asked me to lead a full redesign.

Rather than treat these as two separate projects, I used the navigation rebuild as the new design system’s proof of concept.

Problem Statement

Goodself's designers and developers need a single, reliable source of truth for typography, components, and colour. Undocumented, inconsistent, non-compliant UI slowed every handoff and made the product harder to use.

Solution

I worked on an audit-first, incrementally-shipped system, prioritized by visibility and risk, and tested as a live proof of concept through a navigation redesign.

Design System Audit

Typography

Typography got the most rigorous audit as it was the most jarring problem across desktop and mobile; some mobile interfaces had font sizes as small as 6px.

I built a structured tracking sheet cataloguing every typographic style in production including details such as mobile/desktop sizes, weight, line height, location, frequency, and Figma name.

The results surfaced 50+ distinct type styles in use, many unnamed and never formally added to any system.

Why the Old Library Failed

Although an official typography library technically existed, team adoption was low because the library itself was ambiguous and disconnected from production.

Designer Pain Points: Generic, un-prefixed names like Heading 4 gave no indication whether a style belonged to Goodself's official system or was leftover noise from an old file. Because mobile styles had no prefix at all while desktop used Web/, designers couldn't tell canonical styles from legacy ones, leading them to ignore the library, detach styles, or create one-off text layers.

Developer Pain Points: The Figma names were completely disconnected from the codebase. Because style names in design didn't use the same "language" as the code, engineers had to manually translate and guess which design style matched which code snippet every time they built a screen.

Spacing

As part of the same audit, I documented frequently used spacing values across the app, giving the team a factual baseline. The next step would be defining the spacing tokens in Figma alongside the customer system rollout.

Components and Colour

Cataloguing the official library fully upfront would have meant documenting a system the team wasn't even using; components and colour were harder to audit the same way, because so little of what was in production was pulled from the current design library.

Designers routinely defaulted to legacy or deprecated components instead, or guessed at the correct one. Several elements also existed only as one-off variants rather than reusable components.

Some of the colour problems weren't even visible through Figma's own tools: not every instance of our orange used a shared colour style, and several were applied as raw, one-off hex values.

Navigation

During onboarding, feedback from new hires surfaced that Goodself's navigation, especially on desktop, was clunky and unintuitive. In testing sessions, this friction visibly slowed people down as they searched for essential flows. Assessing the existing navigation against standard usability heuristics confirmed it was violating well-established principles on all platforms.

Competitive Analysis

I ran a competitive analysis across 9 apps, including healthtech and weight-management apps that shared our audience and constraints. For each, I looked at how many distinct greys and blacks they used, their mobile text-sizing conventions, how they applied colour to icons and distinguished categories, and which apps used a warm primary colour more effectively than we used our orange.

This research provided an evidence-based target for refining our orange and gave us a benchmark of best industry standards.

Scoping by Feasibility

Instead of waiting to launch an all-or-nothing design system, I iterated and shipped items by feasibility: typography, spacing, and navigation were prioritized first and are fully shipped; icons are designed, proposed, and live on the new navigation, rolling out to legacy surfaces as they're touched; components are being resolved incrementally through an ongoing ticket-based workflow; and colour remains in decision, pending leadership sign-off.

[PRIORITIZATION MATRIX]

Typography

I consolidated the 50+ styles down to 9 canonical typographic styles (with a 12px minimum for captions).

To restore trust and eliminate handoff ambiguity, I overhauled the naming conventions to match production dev shorthand 1:1, and isolated edge cases into dedicated MR/ (Mobile Rare) and DR/ (Desktop Rare) prefixes. The new naming convention gave designers a home for utility styles without polluting the core type scale, while making edge cases instantly searchable for future team audits and style consolidations.

With the new naming convention in place, designers could instantly identify system-approved styles in Figma, while developers could copy token names directly from code and search them in Figma without translation. The new type scale shipped to both Figma and production.

Components

To address accumulated design debt without halting active feature work, I introduced a flag-and-ticket workflow to incrementally fix UI errors alongside feature sprints. By tracing legacy elements back to their source files, I systematically evaluated, refined, and rebuilt the core components shown below.

Spacing

To fix inconsistent spacing across the product, I built a two-tier token system which included a platform-agnostic primitive scale of a 4px base unit (e.g., space-1 through space-8) and a semantic layer of purpose-based tokens (e.g., container-inset) that mapped each primitive differently across mobile and desktop based on context.

Using this system alongside the component flag-and-ticket workflow, I systematically updated spacing on high-traffic surfaces including carousels, course cards, and popups. I ensured every refactored component shipped with complete state coverage and clear ticket documentation, providing the team with a clear migration path and a single source of truth.

Usability Testing

To gauge the overall website experience, I co-designed the usability test questions and ran the synthesis session, grouping feedback from 12 participants across three task areas: first impressions, wayfinding, and the talent search experience.

Overall sentiment was positive and the “would recommend to a friend” score came in at 4.3 out of 5. But two critical issues emerged from the affinity mapping session: a 73% misclick rate on location, and a recurring sense that pages felt disconnected rather than part of one system.

["ON A SCALE OF 1-5, HOW LIKELY ARE YOU TO RECOMMEND THE WEBSITE TO A FRIEND?"]

The Project Page A/B Test

To resolve layout direction for student profiles, we ran an A/B preference test.

Screen A won the preference vote by a small margin. Participants also preferred specific details from Screen B, most notably placing the project title above the hero image, which made it immediately clear they were meant to scroll.

Between both layouts, users reported that project descriptions felt overwhelming as a single dense block, and images felt too large, making scrolling tedious. The overall feedback pointed to wanting content broken into smaller pieces so text and visuals could build naturally upon scrolling, leading to a rework of the project page layout in Phase 3 (Screen C).

Addressing Key Pain Points

We worked systematically to address other pain points from the usability test.

Information Hierarchy & Wayfinding

The Problem: A 73% misclick rate occurred as location details were buried in the footer.

The Fix: Made location details more visible in the updated navbar and footer, with additional touchpoints linking out to the Google Maps & Eventbrite.

Typography & Legibility

The Problem: Several testers flagged that the brand's signature glow effect on the display typeface, combined with tight kerning and motion, made body text difficult to read.

The Fix: Adjusted the colour values to soften the glow radius, improving readability and achieving WCAG compliance without compromising brand identity.

Spacing & Layout Cohesion

The Problem: Excessive negative space caused related data points to feel visually disconnected. Inconsistent use of spacing and border radius across designers also made the overall design feel disjointed.

The Fix: Reworked sections with excessive negative space. Established uniform spacing and radius standards by section within the design system, and tightened layout bounding boxes to group related items by proximity.

Scaling Fixes into the Design System

I worked with the web team to bake these usability fixes directly into our global design system, so a fix applied to one layout/section became standard practice across all others. By pushing updates through the design system and component library rather than one-off page edits, we sped up the update process, eliminated visual drift, and protected our hard launch timeline.

Icons

The old system pulled inconsistently from multiple icon libraries with no standard weights or fill states. I standardized the entire application onto a single icon library with standardized weights and fills, resolving long-standing legibility issues across the product.

Colour

Through the same ticketed workflow, I mapped off-value orange hexes in production to our primary and secondary oranges to eliminate scattered shades and set a clean baseline. While this interim fix didn't resolve the core orange's underlying WCAG contrast issues, enforcing a single standard across production was necessary to clear design debt for a future system-wide colour update.

To solve the accessibility gap, I mapped out a broader palette concept with intentional tint stacks and a full accessibility matrix, testing the colours directly on key components to ensure they aligned with the brand in context. The larger colour-story decision is still pending, presented to leadership as the proposal for the system's next phase.

Final Outcomes

When I flagged the scale of these problems through testing and audits, it became a cross-functional initiative; designers coordinated to clear the file of legacy components entirely. Every designer I've talked to since states it's dramatically easier to work in, with components properly grouped and file hygiene for the first time.

Developers benefited too: with 1:1 token parity between Figma and the codebase, code and design finally spoke the same language, cutting the guesswork and back-and-forth that used to define every handoff.

I shipped the redesigned navigation live, unified the app onto a single accessible orange, and reduced typographic fragmentation by over 85% consolidating 50+ loose text styles into 9 tokens, all now live in production. The navigation redesign in particular made a visible difference: people found the product noticeably easier to navigate, and the new interface was well-received enough that it came up in conversations with investors.

An expanded, WCAG-compliant brand colour palette is fully documented and proposed as the system's next phase.

Building & QA

MY ROLE IN THE BUILD

Drawing on my experience with frameworks like Tailwind, I checked implementation details and led QA; I verified each page matched Figma across mobile, tablet and desktop, standardized layouts where appropriate, built the carousel with Javascript, and occasionally updated CMS content. With the designs finalized, my role was keeping the web team aligned and making sure our work stayed consistent with the quality we'd set out to deliver.

Standards before building

Before anyone started building, Sam Lampano and I led a team readout on Webflow conventions and best practices. Getting this agreed on upfront meant 8 people building in the same file wouldn't quietly drift into 8 different conventions.

What went smoothly

Webflow's flex-based layout combined with our utility-class system made most of the build straightforward. Additionally, standardizing our units meant responsive behavior mostly "just worked" once a section was built correctly the first time.

Technical Trade-offs

Live vectors rendered inconsistently across browsers, so we initially swapped them for baked gradient images. While this worked for static pages, it broke scalability across 44 dynamic CMS pages. Rather than shipping something inconsistent at scale, I cut gradients from CMS pages entirely.

<<<<<<< HEAD The hardest part of this project was coordination across two different axes.

The Cross-Functional Axis: Teams like branding, external relations, and web development each brought competing priorities and timelines. Success meant acting as the bridge to keep everyone aligned.

The Human Axis: Leading a cohort of peers means navigating unpredictable schedules as students juggle intense academic workloads and life.

I learned that leading well under these constraints requires discarding assumptions. It meant moving away from assuming people automatically knew how to execute a task, and treating project outcomes as a shared responsibility.

Crucially, it taught me to design workflows around people's actual capacity, not just their technical capability. By intentionally checking in on a teammate's emotional and bandwidth before assigning a task, we prevented people from becoming quietly overcommitted. This empathy-driven approach smoothed out our handoffs and kept the team united, engaged, and supported from start to finish.

======= This was the first design system I'd shipped, and doing it while actively shipping feature work changed how I think about ownership. Auditing 50+ untracked text styles and being the one to clean it up an old system taught me the hard way that a design system is a set of decisions everyone after you inherits. The foundation had to hold up before I could build on top of it. Even a seemingly minuscule change, like button sizes, can end up breaking the app and slowing down the time it takes to design and develop new features.

Overall, holding back on production changes such as colour was as much a design decision as anything I shipped, since I made sure that all my changes held up at scale. If I could do this project again, I'd push harder on explicit do's and don'ts documentation, closer to how mature systems such as Carbon document their components.

Underneath it all, this project was less about making the system look impressive and more about designing responsibly and leaving a foundation that future designers and developers could build on with confidence.
