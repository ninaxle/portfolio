Building Trust Through Design Systems at Goodself

Goodself is a healthtech startup that combines social media with evidence-based health information, connecting users with vetted health professionals through interactive streams, courses, and communities. As a UX designer on the product team, I balanced day-to-day feature sprints with building Goodself’s first formal design system.

Overview

When I joined Goodself, a fast-moving healthtech startup, speed had taken priority over scalability. Design decisions lived in individual heads instead of shared documentation, our brand color and typography were fragmented, and most interfaces had never been evaluated against WCAG 2.2 AA.

Around the same time, leadership raised a separate but related problem; new-hire feedback kept flagging Goodself's navigation, especially on desktop, as clunky and unintuitive and asked me to lead a full redesign.

Rather than treat these as two separate projects, I used the navigation rebuild as the new design system’s proof of concept.

The Problem Statement

Goodself's designers and developers need a single, reliable source of truth for typography, components, and color, because inconsistent, non-compliant UI slows handoff and puts user trust at risk.

HOW MIGHT WE

How might we build a single, trustworthy design system for Goodself without halting active feature work to do it?

Competitive Benchmarking

I ran a competitive analysis across 9 apps, including healthtech and weight-management apps that shared our audience and constraints. For each, I looked at how many distinct greys and blacks they used, their mobile text-sizing conventions, how they applied colour to icons and distinguished categories, and which apps used a warm primary colour more effectively than we used our orange.

This research provided an evidence-based target for refining our orange and gave us a benchmark of industry standards to ground our design system audit.

Design System Audit

Typography

Typography got the most rigorous audit as it was the most immediately jarring problem across desktop and mobile; some mobile interfaces had font sizes as small as 6.89px.

I built two structured tracking sheets, one for mobile and one for web, cataloguing every typographic style in production against Current Name, Size, Weight, Line Height, Figma name, Dev-side name, Comments, and Implementation status.

The results surfaced over 55 distinct type styles in use, many unnamed and never formally added to any system.

Why the Old Library Failed

Although an official typography library technically existed, team adoption was low because the library itself was ambiguous and disconnected from production.

For Designers: Generic, un-prefixed names like Heading 4 gave no indication whether a style belonged to Goodself's official system or was just leftover noise from an old file. Because mobile styles had no prefix at all while desktop used Web/, designers couldn't tell canonical styles from legacy ones, leading them to bypass the library, detach styles, or create one-off text layers.

For Developers: The Figma names were completely disconnected from the codebase. Because style names in design didn't use the same "language" as the code, engineers had to manually translate and guess which design style matched which code snippet every time they built a screen.

Spacing

As part of the same audit, I documented frequently used spacing values across the app, giving the team a factual baseline. The next step would be defining the spacing tokens in Figma alongside the customer system rollout.

Components and Colour

Components and color were harder to audit the same way, because so little of what was in production was actually pulled from the current design library.

Designers routinely defaulted to legacy or deprecated components instead, or guessed at the correct one. Several elements also existed only as one-off variants rather than reusable components. Cataloguing the official library fully upfront would have meant documenting a system the team wasn't even using.

Some of the colour problems weren't even visible through Figma's own tools: not every instance of our orange used a shared color style, and several were applied as raw, one-off hex values that a simple style audit wouldn't catch.

The Solution

I worked on an audit-first, incrementally-shipped system, prioritized by visibility and risk, and validated as a live proof of concept through a navigation redesign.

The Solution

Typography

I consolidated the 55+ styles down to 8 canonical typographic styles (with a 12px minimum for body copy).

To restore trust and eliminate handoff ambiguity, I overhauled the naming conventions to match production dev shorthand 1:1, and isolated edge cases into dedicated MR/ (Mobile Rare) and DR/ (Desktop Rare) sub-buckets.

The new naming convention gave designers a home for utility styles without polluting the core type scale, while making edge cases instantly searchable (MR/*) for future team audits and style consolidations.

With the new naming convention in place, designers could instantly identify system-approved styles in Figma, while developers could copy token names directly from code and search them in Figma without translation. The new type scale shipped to both Figma and production.

Components

To address accumulated design debt without halting active feature work, I introduced a flag-and-ticket workflow. Instead of attempting a massive overhaul, we systematically flagged UI errors in Figma and live builds, converting them into individual backlog tickets to fix incrementally alongside feature sprints. Designers claimed specific sections of the product, tracing legacy components back to their source files to evaluate, refine, or deprecate redundant patterns.

Spacing

I redesigned and standardized our highest-traffic surfaces, including carousels, course cards, and popups, fixing inconsistent content spacing, incorrect padding, and touch targets that fell short of the 44×44px minimum. Every updated component was delivered with full state coverage (default, hover, active, disabled, error) and documented in Notion with cross-reference tables mapping legacy styles to new design tokens.

Icons

The old system pulled inconsistently from multiple icon libraries with no standard weights or fill states. I consolidated the entire application onto a single icon library with standardized weights and fills, resolving long-standing legibility issues across the product.

Colour

As part of the same ticket-based process, I brought all modules and buttons into consistent sizing and consolidated them onto a single, WCAG 2.2 AA compliant orange, replacing the scattered shades and untracked hex values the audit had surfaced.

I mapped out a broader palette concept with intentional tint stacks and a full accessibility matrix, and used competitive benchmarking as a reference point to draft multiple compliant color story directions for the brand more broadly: one that kept our existing yellow as a lower-disruption option, alongside bolder alternatives including blue, black, and magenta. That larger color-story decision is still pending, presented to leadership as the proposal for the system's next phase.

Navigation Redesign as Pilot

During onboarding, feedback from new hires surfaced another clear usability problem: Goodself's navigation, especially on desktop, was clunky and unintuitive. In testing sessions, this friction visibly slowed people down as they searched for essential flows. Assessing the existing navigation against standard usability heuristics confirmed it was violating well-established principles on both platforms.

Impact

What started as my personal tracking sheet became a cross-functional initiative once I shared the Figma file, designers and developers began co-owning rollout constraints without me prompting it.

Shipped to Production: Reduced typographic fragmentation by over 85% (consolidating 55+ loose styles into 8 canonical tokens), unified the app onto a single WCAG-compliant orange, and shipped the redesigned navigation live.

Streamlined Design Workflow: Clear component organization and token taxonomy (MC/, DC/) eliminated daily friction. Designers no longer waste time deciphering ambiguous files; they know exactly which canonical button, popup, or form style to select and iterate on.

Cut Handoff Friction: Achieving 1:1 token parity between Figma and the codebase, alongside documented component states and accessibility specs, drastically reduced back-and-forth communication between design and engineering.

Scoped Next Phase: Spacing tokens and an expanded WCAG-compliant brand color palette are fully documented and proposed as the system's next phase.

Key Takeaways

Accessibility as a foundation. Building WCAG standards into components from day one made quality structural, rather than something to fix later.

Audits create shared reality. A number like "55 unnamed styles" turns a subjective debate into an indisputable baseline for change.

Sequencing by urgency. Prioritizing the most visible problems, typography and navigation, delivered value immediately, while the higher-risk, harder-to-reverse change (color) was deliberately sequenced for the next phase.

What I'd Do Differently

While I researched mature systems for structural inspiration, I would now push documentation even further by studying frameworks like Apple's HIG, Spark, and IBM's Carbon to include explicit "Do's and Don'ts" and code integration examples directly beside each component card.

Because I relied on internal new-hire feedback as a proxy for feedback, I didn't have direct access to external users during the navigation redesign. While the redesign successfully resolved the complaints that triggered the project, I would push in the future for lightweight moderated usability sessions with actual health professionals to validate borrowed navigation patterns against our specific audience.
